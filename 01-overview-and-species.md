Summary of Purpose

The Atlantic sea scallop is one of the most valuable fisheries in the United States (U.S.). The National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS) is required to assess the status of sea scallop populations and set catch limits for sea scallops in Atlantic U.S. waters, typically implementing annual or biennial specifications through the New England Fishery Management Council (NEFMC). Monitoring the population status of sea scallops is a top priority for the Population & Ecosystems Monitoring & Analysis Division (PEMAD) at the Northeast Fisheries Science Center (NEFSC). 

The HABitat CAMera system (HABCAM) is a non-invasive, underwater imaging vehicle towed from the stern of research vessels approximately 2 meters above the seafloor, capturing high-resolution, overlapping stereo images. The camera array is additionally equipped with a compass, CTD, sonar and sensors that survey the seafloor, which can be used for estimates of species abundance, habitat characterization, and biodiversity - giving a holistic view of benthic taxa. This vehicle has been used extensively for surveying the sea scallop resource on Georges Bank and the Mid-Atlantic Bight, as well as collecting data on finfish and habitat. 

Why We Survey

Atlantic sea scallops are one of the largest commercial fisheries in the United States. In 2023 the value of the fishery was $359.5 million.

The Magnuson–Stevens Act fosters the long-term biological and economic sustainability of marine fisheries, including the scallop fishery. Under this act, we help:
Prevent overfishing
Rebuild overfished stocks
Increase long-term economic and social benefits derived from fishing
Ensure a safe and sustainable supply of seafood
Protect habitat that fishery species need to spawn, breed, feed, and grow to maturity
Fisheries independent data from the HabCam Survey is used in the sea scallop stock assessment to infer how the stock size and composition has changed over time. Since sea scallops live on the sea floor, the HabCam’s towed camera system is an ideal method for understanding the abundance and distribution of this species.
The survey began as a collaboration between the Northeast Fisheries Science Center and the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institute in 2004 through a Cooperative Institute of the North Atlantic Region agreement. The survey was then transitioned to the Northeast Fisheries Science Center in 2012 as part of the Ecosystems Surveys enterprise. The survey typically runs between April and June sampling the Mid-Atlantic Bight and Georges Bank. On average, the survey is completed over two legs, each spanning 9 to 10 days.

For many years, the HabCam Survey and the Scallop Dredge Survey were integrated over three survey legs aboard the R/V Hugh Sharp. This vessel is homeported in Lewes, Delaware, at the University of Delaware. With the transition of the Scallop Dredge Survey to an industry vessel in 2023, the HabCam Survey is now completed on the NOAA Ship Henry Bigelow.
The primary equipment of the HabCam Survey is the HabCam vehicle. The vehicle houses a suite of sensors and electronics mounted on a frame that is towed behind the ship via a fiber optic cable. HabCam sensors include stereo cameras and strobes for seabed imaging, and sensors for measuring:
Conductivity
Temperature
Pressure
Oxygen concentration
Turbidity
Chlorophyll
Fluorescent dissolved organic matter
HabCam also uses a side-scan sonar to visualize the seabed and a forward-looking sonar for object avoidance. We tow the vehicle between 5 to 7 knots and it flies approximately 2 meters above the seabed. A pilot manually pays cable in and out to ensure clear visibility of the seafloor and achieve the desired height above the seabed. The stereo imaging capability allows for precise measurement of sea scallop size. Scientists annotate images and, using the estimated field of view, derive sea scallop standardized abundances and size compositions.
Data Use
The data is used to develop indices of abundance and size composition of Atlantic sea scallop for the  stock assessments. The stereo imaging capability allows for precise measurement of sea scallop size . Additional fisheries and ecosystem data collected from the images is also processed for use in future stock and ecosystem assessment needs.
A number of other surveys collect data on scallop distribution, abundance, and size. These surveys are supported by the Center’s Scallop Dredge survey and the Research Set Aside Program. They include dredge, video drop camera, and an additional HabCam survey. The intent of these multiple surveys and survey methods is to increase the accuracy and precision of the sea scallop assessment through thorough fisheries independent surveys.
