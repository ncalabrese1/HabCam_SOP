## Summary of Purpose

The Atlantic sea scallop is one of the most valuable fisheries in the United States (U.S.)[cite: 2]. The National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS) is required to assess the status of sea scallop populations and set catch limits for sea scallops in Atlantic U.S. waters, typically implementing annual or biennial specifications through the New England Fishery Management Council (NEFMC)[cite: 2]. Monitoring the population status of sea scallops is a top priority for the Population & Ecosystems Monitoring & Analysis Division (PEMAD) at the Northeast Fisheries Science Center (NEFSC)[cite: 2].

The HABitat CAMera system (HABCAM) is a non-invasive, underwater imaging vehicle towed from the stern of research vessels approximately 2 meters above the seafloor, capturing high-resolution, overlapping stereo images[cite: 2]. The camera array is additionally equipped with a compass, CTD, sonar and sensors that survey the seafloor, which can be used for estimates of species abundance, habitat characterization, and biodiversity—giving a holistic view of benthic taxa[cite: 2]. This vehicle has been used extensively for surveying the sea scallop resource on Georges Bank and the Mid-Atlantic Bight, as well as collecting data on finfish and habitat[cite: 2].

## Why We Survey

Atlantic sea scallops are one of the largest commercial fisheries in the United States[cite: 2]. In 2023, the value of the fishery was $359.5 million[cite: 2].

The Magnuson–Stevens Act fosters the long-term biological and economic sustainability of marine fisheries, including the scallop fishery[cite: 2]. Under this act, we help:
* Prevent overfishing[cite: 2]
* Rebuild overfished stocks[cite: 2]
* Increase long-term economic and social benefits derived from fishing[cite: 2]
* Ensure a safe and sustainable supply of seafood[cite: 2]
* Protect habitat that fishery species need to spawn, breed, feed, and grow to maturity[cite: 2]

Fisheries-independent data from the HabCam Survey is used in the sea scallop stock assessment to infer how the stock size and composition has changed over time[cite: 2]. Since sea scallops live on the sea floor, the HabCam’s towed camera system is an ideal method for understanding the abundance and distribution of this species[cite: 2].

The survey began as a collaboration between the Northeast Fisheries Science Center and the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution in 2004 through a Cooperative Institute of the North Atlantic Region agreement[cite: 2]. The survey was transitioned to the Northeast Fisheries Science Center in 2012 as part of the Ecosystems Surveys enterprise[cite: 2]. The survey typically runs between April and June sampling the Mid-Atlantic Bight and Georges Bank over two legs, each spanning 9 to 10 days[cite: 2].

For many years, the HabCam Survey and the Scallop Dredge Survey were integrated over three survey legs aboard the R/V *Hugh Sharp* homeported in Lewes, Delaware[cite: 2]. With the transition of the Scallop Dredge Survey to an industry vessel in 2023, the HabCam Survey is now completed on the *NOAA Ship Henry B. Bigelow*[cite: 2].

The primary equipment of the HabCam Survey is the HabCam vehicle[cite: 2]. The vehicle houses a suite of sensors and electronics mounted on a frame towed behind the ship via a fiber optic cable[cite: 2]. HabCam sensors include stereo cameras and strobes for seabed imaging, and sensors for measuring:
* Conductivity[cite: 2]
* Temperature[cite: 2]
* Pressure[cite: 2]
* Oxygen concentration[cite: 2]
* Turbidity[cite: 2]
* Chlorophyll[cite: 2]
* Fluorescent dissolved organic matter (fDOM)[cite: 2]

HabCam also uses a side-scan sonar to visualize the seabed and a forward-looking sonar for object avoidance[cite: 2]. We tow the vehicle between 5 to 7 knots and it flies approximately 2 meters above the seabed[cite: 2]. A pilot manually pays cable in and out to ensure clear visibility of the seafloor and achieve the desired height above the seabed[cite: 2]. Scientists annotate images and, using the estimated field of view, derive sea scallop standardized abundances and size compositions[cite: 2].

<br>

## Data Use

The data is used to develop indices of abundance and size composition of Atlantic sea scallop for the stock assessments[cite: 2]. Additional fisheries and ecosystem data collected from the images is also processed for use in future stock and ecosystem assessment needs[cite: 2].

A number of other surveys collect data on scallop distribution, abundance, and size supported by the Center's Scallop Dredge survey and the Research Set Aside Program (including dredge, video drop camera, and an additional HabCam survey)[cite: 2]. The intent of these multiple surveys is to increase the accuracy and precision of the sea scallop assessment through thorough fisheries-independent surveys[cite: 2].

# About the Species: The Atlantic Sea Scallop

<img src="images/Scallop%20Shell.jpg" alt="Atlantic Sea Scallop Shell" width="400"/>

*Atlantic Sea Scallop (Placopecten magellanicus)*

</div>

<br>

## Overview & Population Status

The primary Atlantic sea scallop fishery operates along the Atlantic coast from the Mid-Atlantic to the US/Canada border using predominantly paired or single scallop dredges and trawl gear[cite: 2]. Most vessels land scallops as shucked meats (the adductor muscle), though some land whole scallops[cite: 2]. Implementing regulations are found at **50 CFR part 648 subpart D**[cite: 2].

According to the 2020 stock assessment, Atlantic sea scallop is **not overfished** and **not subject to overfishing**[cite: 2].

<br>

## Appearance & Biology

* **Appearance:** Scallops are bivalves held together by an adductor muscle[cite: 2]. Shells are saucer-shaped with smooth, scalloped edges[cite: 2]. The upper shell is reddish-pink/brown and the lower shell is white or cream (5–10% are albinos)[cite: 2].
* **Lifespan:** Can live up to 20 years, growing rapidly during their first few years up to ~6 inches[cite: 2].
* **Reproduction:** Reproduce by age 2, producing hundreds of millions of eggs per year during late summer/early fall or spring spawning[cite: 2]. Larvae remain in the water column for 4–6 weeks before settling[cite: 2].
* **Locomotion:** Propel themselves through the water by snapping top and bottom shells open and shut to escape predators like sea stars[cite: 2].

## Habitat, Range & Management

Atlantic sea scallops are found in the Northwest Atlantic Ocean from Newfoundland to Cape Hatteras, NC[cite: 2]. Adult scallops live in dense "beds" on sandy or gravelly ocean floors at depths of 100 to 300 feet on Georges Bank and the Mid-Atlantic[cite: 2].

<div align="center">

<img src="images/Stock%20Area.jpg" alt="US and Canadian Scallop Resource Map" width="600"/>

<br>

NOAA Fisheries and the New England Fishery Management Council manage the fishery under the **Atlantic Sea Scallop Fishery Management Plan** via Total Allowable Catch (TAC), crew limits, rotational closures, Vessel Monitoring Systems (VMS), and Individual Fishing Quotas (IFQs)[cite: 2].

<br>

## Harvest & Gear Impacts

In 2023, commercial landings totaled **27.4 million pounds** of meats valued at **$360 million**[cite: 2]. Fishermen primarily use turtle deflector-style dredges equipped with 4-inch rings to increase efficiency and allow undersized scallops to escape[cite: 2].
