# Why We Survey

Atlantic sea scallops are one of the largest commercial fisheries in the United States. In 2023, the value of the fishery was $359.5 million.

The Magnuson–Stevens Act fosters the long-term biological and economic sustainability of marine fisheries, including the scallop fishery. Under this act, we help:
* Prevent overfishing
* Rebuild overfished stocks
* Increase long-term economic and social benefits derived from fishing
* Ensure a safe and sustainable supply of seafood
* Protect habitat that fishery species need to spawn, breed, feed, and grow to maturity

Fisheries-independent data from the HabCam Survey is used in the sea scallop stock assessment to infer how the stock size and composition has changed over time. Since sea scallops live on the sea floor, the HabCam’s towed camera system is an ideal method for understanding the abundance and distribution of this species.

The survey began as a collaboration between the Northeast Fisheries Science Center and the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution in 2004 through a Cooperative Institute of the North Atlantic Region agreement. The survey was transitioned to the Northeast Fisheries Science Center in 2012 as part of the Ecosystems Surveys enterprise. The survey typically runs between April and June sampling the Mid-Atlantic Bight and Georges Bank over two legs, each spanning 9 to 10 days.

For many years, the HabCam Survey and the Scallop Dredge Survey were integrated over three survey legs aboard the R/V *Hugh Sharp* homeported in Lewes, Delaware. With the transition of the Scallop Dredge Survey to an industry vessel in 2023, the HabCam Survey is now completed on the *NOAA Ship Henry B. Bigelow*.

The primary equipment of the HabCam Survey is the HabCam vehicle. The vehicle houses a suite of sensors and electronics mounted on a frame towed behind the ship via a fiber optic cable. HabCam sensors include stereo cameras and strobes for seabed imaging, and sensors for measuring:
* Conductivity
* Temperature
* Pressure
* Oxygen concentration
* Turbidity
* Chlorophyll
* Fluorescent dissolved organic matter (fDOM)

HabCam also uses a side-scan sonar to visualize the seabed and a forward-looking sonar for object avoidance. We tow the vehicle between 5 to 7 knots and it flies approximately 2 meters above the seabed. A pilot manually pays cable in and out to ensure clear visibility of the seafloor and achieve the desired height above the seabed. Scientists annotate images and, using the estimated field of view, derive sea scallop standardized abundances and size compositions.
